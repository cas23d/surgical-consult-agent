# Getting Testimonials & Clinical Validation

A single testimonial from a peer clinician is worth more than a dozen features you built alone. This guide covers how to gather authentic validation quickly.

## Why Testimonials Matter

Hiring managers in health tech think like this:
- "Chris says he built a clinical AI tool" → skeptical
- "Chris says his tool improved workflow, and here's a surgeon who confirms it" → **credible**

One testimonial solves the "is this real?" question immediately.

## Who to Ask

### Priority 1: Recent Surgery Residents (Your Leverage)
**Why:** They know your context, understand residency pain points, and are quick to respond.

**Who:**
- Current residents at your program
- Residents at other programs who experienced similar issues
- Fellow surgery residents from medical school
- Interns rotating through your previous program

**How to approach:**
"Hey [name], I'm building an AI tool to help with surgical consults. Would you be open to testing it and giving me quick feedback? Should take 5 min to try, and I'd love a quick quote if you think it's useful."

**What to ask for:**
1. 30-second test of the demo
2. One-sentence feedback on what was useful
3. Optional: name and current program for visibility

### Priority 2: Attending Surgeons
**Why:** Attending endorsement carries weight with leadership.

**Who:**
- Attendings from your program
- Mentors you've worked with
- Faculty at your medical school
- Attendings on Twitter/LinkedIn in clinical AI

**How to approach:**
Email/LinkedIn message:
"I've spent the last 2 years building AI tools for surgical workflows. Would you have 10 minutes to see the demo? I'm looking for clinical feedback on whether this actually solves a real problem."

**What to ask for:**
1. Feedback on clinical accuracy
2. Judgment on whether it's solving a real workflow problem
3. Permission to use their name as a reference

### Priority 3: Product/Clinical Teams at Health Tech Companies
**Why:** Endorsement from someone at Abridge/Nabla/Glass signals you understand the landscape.

**Who:**
- Product managers at target companies
- Clinical advisors at health tech startups
- Physicians who are also builders (Twitter/Product Hunt)

**How to approach:**
Direct outreach (Twitter, LinkedIn, or via mutual connection):
"I see you're working on [clinical AI product]. I built a surgical consult agent and would love clinical feedback from someone who gets this space. Do you have 10 min?"

**What to ask for:**
1. Honest assessment vs. competitive products
2. Feedback on gaps in current solutions
3. Optional: a quote about your approach to clinical workflow design

## What to Ask For

### The Perfect Testimonial Format

**Short (1-2 sentences):**
"This tool cut my consult documentation from 10 minutes to 2 minutes without sacrificing accuracy. The exam findings input is exactly what was missing from other tools." — Sarah Chen, MD, Surgery PGY-3

**Medium (3-4 sentences):**
"I tested this on three actual consults during rounds. The triage stage immediately flagged a lactate I almost missed. More importantly, it doesn't try to make decisions for you — it surfaces what matters and lets you lead. That's the right mental model for clinical AI." — Dr. James Wilson, Surgical Oncology

**What makes a good testimonial:**
- Specific detail (not generic praise)
- Mentions actual use or testing
- Addresses a real clinical problem
- Shows understanding of the tool's design philosophy
- Uses the person's actual name and credentials

### What NOT to Ask For

❌ "This is the best clinical AI tool ever!"
❌ "Revolutionary technology!"
❌ "The future of surgery!"

These are useless. They signal the testimonial isn't authentic.

## Template Outreach Message

Copy and customize this:

---

Subject: 10-min feedback on a surgical AI tool I built

Hi [Name],

I've been working on a surgical consult agent designed specifically for the chaos of residency (you know, hour 28 of a shift). It pulls patient data from the EHR, does rapid triage, and generates a copy-paste-ready consult note.

I'd love 10 minutes of feedback from someone who knows surgical workflows. The demo takes 2 minutes to run through. Would you be open to looking at it?

If you think it's solving a real problem, I'd appreciate a quick quote I can use on my portfolio — something honest about what actually works or what's missing.

[Link to demo site]

Thanks,
Christopher

---

## Collecting Feedback

### Email Template (Send After They Test)

"Thanks for looking at the demo. A few quick questions if you have time:

1. What did you think was most useful?
2. What's missing or doesn't quite work?
3. Would you use something like this in your real workflow?
4. [If yes] Would you be open to a short quote for my portfolio? Something like: '[What was useful] — [Your name], [Your title/program]'"

## Timing & Urgency

### Fast Path (2 weeks)
1. **Week 1** — Deploy live demo, record video
2. **Week 1-2** — Reach out to 10-15 residents you know
3. **Week 2** — Compile 3-5 quick quotes, update portfolio

### Better Path (1 month)
1. **Week 1-2** — Deploy, gather feedback from residents
2. **Week 2-3** — Incorporate feedback, improve demo
3. **Week 3-4** — Reach out to 2-3 attendings + health tech contacts
4. **Week 4** — Finalize testimonials, create testimonials page

## Handling Negative Feedback

Some people will say "This doesn't solve my actual problem." This is valuable.

**Use it:** "Dr. [Name] pointed out that the tool doesn't integrate with [X system]. That's a critical gap for real-world adoption."

This shows:
1. You're getting real feedback (not manufactured)
2. You understand limitations
3. You're building toward a production system, not a toy

You don't need everyone to say it's perfect. You need **credible people saying it's solving real problems**.

## Follow-Up with Testimonial Givers

Once you have their testimonial:

1. **Thank them** — Personal email or call
2. **Tag them** — When you post their quote on LinkedIn, tag their profile
3. **Keep them updated** — As you improve the tool, loop them in
4. **Potential long-term** — Ask if they'd be interested in advisory role

These people could become:
- References when you apply for jobs
- Co-authors on a publication about clinical AI
- Collaborators on the next version
- Advocates who recommend you to others

## Final Thought

The best testimonials are from people who are skeptical. They carry weight precisely *because* they're not automatically your fans.

So reach out to that attending who's always been critical. Show them the demo. Ask what's actually broken. If they find value despite high standards, their endorsement is gold.

That's authenticity. That's credibility. That's what will make hiring managers listen.
